Mayflower, mathematical extensions
==================================

1. The adaptive adversary
-------------------------

A committed hider fixes the board first, so the searcher faces an average.
An adaptive hider answers each shot to hurt most while staying consistent
with everything already said, so the searcher faces a worst case that no
distributional assumption can soften.

instance       boards E[T] committed W* adaptive      gap  beta(L)
3x3 {2}            12         4.5000           7     2.50        4
4x3 {2}            17         5.1176           8     2.88        6
4x4 {2}            24         6.0833          10     3.92        8
4x4 {3}            16         5.6250           8     2.38        5
5x4 {3}            22         6.2273           9     2.77        6
4x4 {2,2}         224         8.6696          12     3.33        -
4x4 {3,2}         264         8.7538          12     3.25        -

For a lone ship the worst case sits above beta(L), the shots that guarantee
first contact, by the cost of finishing the ship once found. The margin is
not a fixed offset: the adversary also chooses which way the ship runs.

3. The adaptivity gap
---------------------

What does feedback buy? A non-adaptive player fixes the order of the cells
before play and never looks at an answer. An adaptive player sees each
outcome before choosing again. Both are solved exactly here, so the ratio
is a measurement rather than a bound.

instance      boards    adaptive fixed order greedy order      gap   ratio
3x3 {2}           12      4.5000      5.9167      6.0000   1.4167  1.3148
4x3 {2}           17      5.1176      7.4706      7.7059   2.3529  1.4598
4x4 {2}           24      6.0833      9.5833      9.7500   3.5000  1.5753
4x4 {3}           16      5.6250     10.8750     11.0000   5.2500  1.9333
4x4 {4}            8      6.0000     11.7500     11.8750   5.7500  1.9583
5x4 {3}           22      6.2273     13.0455     13.3636   6.8182  2.0949
4x4 {2,2}        224      8.6696     12.4866     12.5312   3.8170  1.4403
4x4 {3,2}        264      8.7538     13.1098     13.3182   4.3561  1.4976
5x4 {3,2}        510           -     15.8902     16.1765        -       -
5x4 {4,3,2}     2520           -     18.1437     18.5147        -       -

The fixed order column is the exact optimum over all n! orders, reached
through the subset lattice: an order's clearing time depends on its prefixes
as sets, so the best order is the best chain, and 2^n beats n!.

Feedback is worth most against a lone ship. One 3-ship on 5x4 costs 6.23
shots with feedback and 13.05 without, a ratio of 2.09, the largest here.
Fleets score lower: 4x4 {2,2} gives 1.44 and 4x4 {3,2} gives 1.50. What
feedback buys is the right to skip cells, and a fleet covering more of the
board leaves fewer worth skipping.

The fixed-order column shows the same thing from the other side. On 5x4 it
runs 13.05, 15.89 and 18.14 of 20 cells as the fleet grows, so a player
with no feedback ends up shooting nearly the whole board. That column stops
at 20 cells because the lattice is 2^n; the adaptive column stops at 264
boards, which is the belief MDP and a much harder wall.

The greedy order, taking the cell that covers the most configurations still
uncovered, lands within 3.2% of the optimum on every instance here. The
familiar 4-approximation covers min-sum set cover, where a set is paid for
at its first covered element. This objective waits for the last one, which
is the K(S)=|S| case of generalized min-sum set cover and carries no such
guarantee, so 3.2% is an observation about these instances. See
docs/COMPLEXITY.md.

4. What row and column sums cost
--------------------------------

Bimaru is this board with the occupied count of every row and column given.
The sweep runs column-major, so a column sum is settled inside its column:
one counter of 0..H, checked and cleared at the boundary, multiplying the
state by H+1. A row sum accumulates the whole way across, so every row
counter has to be carried at once, and the multiplier is the number of
distinct partial row-count vectors a cut can see.

instance        boards    cut    row vectors    column sums
4x4 {3,2}          264      3             82              5
5x5 {4,3,2}       9024      4            882              6
6x6 {4,3,2}      53624      4           2338              7
6x6 {4,3,3,2}   633432      5           8675              7

  10x10 {5,4,3,3,2} at the half-board cut, upper bound 5044260 row vectors.
  The peak profile state is 376,735, so the product is 1.9e12 and the sweep
  is finished.

  So the two halves of Bimaru's input split cleanly. Column sums are nearly
  free in this sweep direction and row sums are not, and transposing the
  sweep only swaps which half is which. Sevenster's NP-completeness proof
  for the puzzle sits on the other side of that split, so the plan's
  estimate of one day and a hundred lines held only for the free half.

5. Salvo, and where the approach stops
--------------------------------------

Firing k cells and hearing only how many hit leaves the record ambiguous
whenever the answer is neither 0 nor k. The posterior becomes a union of
constraint sets, each needing its own sweep. Dead branches drop out, so the
growth rate is worth measuring rather than bounding.

Shots are random here, so what follows is a property of the rules and not
of a policy. Classic play is the k=1 row, where the answer names the cell.

  4x4 {3,2}, 264 boards, 60 games per row
    k    turns     split  peak union  sweeps/game vs classic   capped
    1     13.7       0.0           1         13.7        1.0        0
    2      7.3       3.8           9         28.2        1.9        0
    3      4.9       3.5          17         47.6        3.2        0
    4      3.8       3.2          41        103.6        6.8        0
    5      2.9       2.7          45        165.8       11.4        0

  5x5 {4,3,2}, 9024 boards, 60 games per row
    k    turns     split  peak union  sweeps/game vs classic   capped
    1     23.6       0.0           1         23.6        1.0        0
    2     11.5       5.8          19         59.8        2.6        0
    3      7.9       5.8          63        190.7        8.1        0
    4      5.9       4.9         124        479.8       20.3        0
    5      4.8       4.5         260       1363.1       56.2        0

  k=1 is one sweep per shot by definition. k=2 costs a few times that and
  survives. Past that the per-turn fan-out is C(k,h), which peaks at the
  middle of the row, and it multiplies across turns.

  The union stays well under the product of the fan-outs because most
  assignments contradict the fleet within a turn or two, which is the only
  reason k=2 is affordable. Nothing in the profile state merges branches:
  two of them disagree about cells the sweep has already passed, so the
  boundary carries no record of the disagreement.

  Real salvo fires one shot per surviving ship, so it opens at k=5 and
  shortens as ships sink. The k=5 row prices the opening.

6. Noisy Battleship
-------------------

Every answer is flipped with probability eps. The likelihood of a board
after t shots is (1-eps)^(t-m) eps^m in its mismatch count m, so the
posterior is exp(-beta m) with beta = ln((1-eps)/eps). Noise is a
temperature and the truthful game is the zero-temperature limit.

Shots pick a uniform cell with replacement, so each one is a single use of
a binary symmetric channel and the capacity bound applies directly.

  4x4 {3,2}, 264 boards, H0 = 8.0444 bits, 400 trials per row
      eps     beta   capacity  shots used      bound    ratio
   0.0005     7.60     0.9938        20.0        8.1     2.47
   0.0100     4.60     0.9192        27.0        8.8     3.09
   0.0500     2.94     0.7136        40.3       11.3     3.57
   0.1000     2.20     0.5310        57.2       15.1     3.77
   0.2000     1.39     0.2781       111.3       28.9     3.85
   0.3000     0.85     0.1187       255.7       67.8     3.77

  5x5 {4,3,2}, 9024 boards, H0 = 13.1396 bits, 60 trials per row
      eps     beta   capacity  shots used      bound    ratio
   0.0005     7.60     0.9938        36.4       13.2     2.76
   0.0100     4.60     0.9192        57.7       14.3     4.04
   0.0500     2.94     0.7136        67.4       18.4     3.66
   0.1000     2.20     0.5310        98.4       24.7     3.98
   0.2000     1.39     0.2781       202.8       47.3     4.29
   0.3000     0.85     0.1187       472.2      110.7     4.27

  Boltzmann identity, 4x4 {3,2} after 25 noisy shots at eps=0.13:
  largest posterior difference between the likelihood product and
  exp(-beta m)/Z is 1.665e-15.

  Shots used sits above the capacity bound at every noise level, which is
  expected: a uniform random cell is not a capacity-achieving input. The
  ratio is the interesting column, since it separates what the channel
  costs from what the shot choice costs.

  Scaling this to 10x10 needs a weighted sweep, which the engine does not
  have. The counting path carries uint64 exact counts throughout, and a
  noisy posterior needs floating-point weights on the transitions, so it
  is a second pass rather than a flag. Everything above is exact by
  enumeration and stops where enumeration stops.
